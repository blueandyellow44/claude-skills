# Critique method: the six dimensions

Grade each dimension from the craft sheet plus a close reading. Pass or revise, a one-line reason, and
the quoted lines behind every revise. The sheet points; the reading decides.

## 1. Line aliveness

Is every sentence earning its place? Look for throat-clearing ("There is a sense in which..."),
sentences that restate the one before, filler clauses that add no information or pressure, and
distancing filters ("I realized that...", "I noticed...") that hold the reader one step back from the
thing itself.

A sentence that could be cut with no loss is a flag. The move is `cut`.

**The filter-word carve-out.** The sheet's filter count is a candidate list. Flag a filter only when it
distances the reader from something the draft then fails to show. "I realized the invoice had been
wrong for six months" stays if the realization is the news. Adjudicate each one.

## 2. Over-explanation

The highest-value catch. After an example or image lands, does the draft then explain it: name the
feeling, spell out the lesson, tell the reader what to conclude? The redundant half is almost always
the second half of the sentence or the sentence after the example.

> We cut the weekly meeting and nobody noticed for a month. That tells you how much the meeting was
> really doing.

The second sentence says what the first already proved. The move is `cut the gloss`.

**Sentimentality is its twin.** A tender closing line that states the feeling the story already
earned ("and that is when I knew it had all been worth it") is over-explanation in a softer voice.

## 3. Concreteness

Does the draft reach for an abstraction ("essentially", "the dynamic", "a kind of", "at its core",
"in many ways") where a specific example, number, name, or scene belongs? The sheet's ABSTRACTION LEAK
list points at candidates.

Flag the abstraction and name what kind of specific is missing. **Never supply the specific yourself**
unless it is already in the writer's material. The move is `concretize from <where>` or
`ask the writer for the real example`.

## 4. Rhythm

The sheet reports mean and spread of sentence length. A low spread over many sentences means a drone:
every sentence the same length and shape. Read a paragraph in your head. Does it move, short against
long, or does it hum?

Flag the flat run by location and say where a short sentence should break it, or where two short ones
should become one. The move is `vary`. Also watch the opposite failure: a new house rhythm of clipped
fragments, repeated until it is its own tic.

## 5. Figurative landing

Does each metaphor or comparison land: apt, fresh, unmixed? A dead or mixed figure reads worse than a
plain statement. "A tapestry of challenges" and "the engine that drives the journey" are both flags.
The move is `sharpen` or `cut to plain`.

## 6. Verdict landings and polish

The most common tell in drafts that were written with or by a model, and in drafts by writers who
learned from them. Four related patterns:

- **Verdict landings.** Paragraph after paragraph ends on a neat conclusion: a balanced antithesis, a
  short aphorism, "That is the whole point." One is craft. The sheet's landing rate and runs of three
  show clustering. The flag is the cluster, not the single good line.
- **Negation reveals.** "This is not a tooling problem. It is a habit problem." The governing test is
  whether the negated idea was ever live for the reader. If nobody thought it was a tooling problem, the
  negation is a stage prop. If the reader really was thinking it, the construction is doing work and
  stays.
- **Diction frequency.** A considered word used three or more times ("quiet", "weight", "genuinely")
  stops reading as considered. The fix is a cull across the whole piece, never one swap. Report the
  count before and after.
- **Closing suspicion.** Endings over-polish. A last line that sums up, labels the lesson, or turns
  "not X but Y" seals the piece instead of ending it. The strongest endings usually stop one sentence
  earlier than the draft does.

The moves here are `cut the landing`, `stop one sentence earlier`, `cull the family`, and
`let the example end the paragraph`.

## How to grade

- Quote the line for every revise. No quote, no flag.
- Stock phrases from the sheet are candidates for dimensions 1 and 5, not automatic flags.
- A deliberate choice the writer can defend (an earned repetition, a hard close that is funny or
  brutal) is not a defect. When unsure, tag it `[taste]` and let the writer decide.
- Confidence per dimension stays low until `lessons.md` holds overrides from this writer.

## What the critic never does

- Rewrite a line.
- Grade facts, structure, argument quality, or grammar.
- Invent an example, number, quote, or detail.
- Flag plain, blunt, or conversational sentences for being plain.
