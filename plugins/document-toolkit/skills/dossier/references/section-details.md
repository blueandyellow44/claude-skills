# Dossier, section-by-section writing guidance

The per-section detail for the 12 dossier sections. Load this when you START WRITING the document (after research is compiled), not during research. The section map (which sections appear in quick vs deep) lives in SKILL.md.

### Section Details

#### 1. Cover Page

Clean, minimal. The subject's name large and centered. Below it: their current
title and organization. Bottom of the page: date prepared, use case label
(e.g., "Meeting Prep," "Hiring Manager Research"), and "Prepared for [the user's name]."

Use the org's brand primary color for the name. Keep it typographic, no clip art,
no stock imagery, no decorative borders.

#### 2. At a Glance

A quick-reference card with 5-7 key facts. This is the "read this on your phone
in the elevator" section. Format as a single card with short, scannable lines:

- Current role and tenure
- Previous most notable role
- Education (degree + institution, one line)
- Location
- One sentence on what they are known for
- One sentence on what they care about right now

#### 3. Background

Narrative paragraph(s), not a resume dump. Tell the career story: where they
started, key inflection points, how they ended up where they are. Focus on
the arc and the pattern, what kind of career has this person built?

For **job application research**: emphasize what this person values in hires
based on their own career choices and stated priorities.

#### 4. Current Role & Org Context

What does the organization do? Where does this person sit in it? How long
have they been in this role? What was the org doing before they arrived vs.
now? Any recent org changes (mergers, layoffs, pivots, funding rounds)?

#### 5. What They Care About

Synthesize from their public statements, writing, talks, and org priorities.
What themes come up repeatedly? What language do they use? What problems are
they trying to solve?

This section is the foundation for the Conversation Hooks and Recommended
Approach sections. Get it right.

#### 6. Conversation Hooks

Specific, actionable things the user can reference to build rapport:

- Shared interests or experiences with the user (cross-reference with what
  Claude knows about the user)
- Recent wins or milestones worth acknowledging
- Topics they are clearly passionate about
- Questions they would enjoy being asked

**Rules:**
- Every hook must be sourced from real information, not inferred
- Do not manufacture connections that do not exist
- If there are no genuine shared interests, say so, forced rapport is worse
  than no rapport
- Rank hooks by naturalness: things that would come up organically in
  conversation first, then things that require a deliberate reference

#### 7. Landmines & Sensitivities (Deep Only)

Things to avoid or handle carefully:

- Public controversies or criticism they have faced
- Org politics (e.g., competing for a role, department conflicts)
- Sensitive topics in their industry or org
- Public missteps or statements they may have walked back
- Topics where they have strong views that could create friction

**Rules:**
- Be factual, not speculative. Only include sourced information.
- Frame as "be aware" not "this person is bad"
- If nothing notable exists, say "No significant public sensitivities identified"
  rather than manufacturing drama

#### 8. Org Landscape (Deep Only)

Who do they report to? Who are their peers at the same level? Any recent
leadership changes? Is the org growing, shrinking, pivoting? What is the
competitive landscape? Who are the other key players the user might
encounter?

#### 9. Recent Activity (Deep Only)

Last 6-12 months of public activity:

- Conference talks or panels
- Published articles or blog posts
- News mentions or press coverage
- Notable social media themes (not individual posts, patterns)
- Awards, promotions, or public milestones

#### 10. Use-Case-Specific Intel (Deep Only)

This section adapts based on the stated use case:

**Meeting Prep:**
- What they likely want from this meeting
- What authority they have to make decisions
- Who else might be in the room and what they care about
- Previous interactions with the user (from their notes, if available)

**Job Application Research:**
- What this person values in candidates (inferred from their career, writing, stated values)
- Interview style signals (structured vs. conversational, technical vs. behavioral)
- What the team/org is currently hiring for and why
- How to frame your experience in terms they will recognize

**Competitor/Peer Analysis:**
- How their work overlaps with or differs from the user's
- Their strengths and gaps relative to the user
- Where they are positioned in the market/field
- What the user can learn from their approach

**Consulting Client Research:**
- Their org's pain points and strategic priorities
- Budget signals (funding, revenue, recent investments)
- Decision-making style (data-driven, relationship-driven, consensus-driven)
- What "success" looks like to them
- How to frame the user's value proposition in their language

#### 11. Recommended Approach (Deep Only)

Concrete guidance on how the user should engage with this person:

- How to open the conversation (specific framing, not generic advice)
- What language/terminology resonates with them
- What to lead with vs. what to save for later
- What to avoid saying or doing
- Recommended ask or call to action

This section should feel like advice from a mentor who knows both people,
not a generic "be professional and listen actively" checklist.

#### 12. Source Notes

Brief section listing where key facts came from. Flag any single-source
claims. Flag any gaps in the research, what you looked for but could not
find.
